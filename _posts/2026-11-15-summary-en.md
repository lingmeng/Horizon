### [SpaceX catches Starship at end of Flight 15](https://www.spacex.com/launches/starship-flight-15) ⭐️ 8.0/10

SpaceX announced that on Flight 15 the orbital Starship completed roughly 22–22.5 hours in orbit \(about 14 revolutions\), deployed 26 Starlink V3 satellites \(three with cameras to inspect thermal tiles\), returned to the Starbase launch site, and was captured by the launch‑tower mechanical arm — the company&\#x27;s first announced tower‑catch of an orbital‑class Starship. The Super Heavy booster did not return to the tower as planned, instead making a controlled splashdown in the Gulf of Mexico and subsequently exploding; SpaceX has not disclosed a cause. Technical details needed to judge operational reusability — including heat‑shield condition, refurbishment requirements, and repeatability of the catch — have not been published.

hackernews · ls612 · Nov 14, 21:22 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50500963)

**「What a &\#x27;tower catch&\#x27; and Starship flight tests are」** A &quot;tower catch&quot; is a recovery method where a launch‑pad‑mounted mechanical arm \(the launch tower&\#x27;s catcher\) grabs a vertically descending stage instead of letting it land, eliminating a propulsive landing or splashdown and reducing refurbishment needs. SpaceX&\#x27;s Starship is a two‑stage, fully reusable architecture whose recent test flights have been progressively validating ascent, orbital insertion, payload deployment, and return trajectories leading up to Flight 15&\#x27;s attempt to catch the orbital stage after it deployed Starlink satellites.

**「Immediate consequence」** If the recovered Starship&\#x27;s heat shield and structure require only limited refurbishment, tower catching an orbital vehicle could materially reduce recovery propellant and landing infrastructure needs and lower per‑flight turn costs; however customers and competitors should treat this as a conditional advance until SpaceX publishes inspection and reflight data. Operators and payload customers should monitor subsequent flights for confirmed mass‑to‑orbit figures, refurbishment timelines, and successful repeated catches before changing procurement or mission assurance assumptions.

**「What commenters say」** Commenters noted the achievement but stressed its significance depends on heat‑shield durability and repeatability \(small\_model and ChuckMcM\), while others compared Starship&\#x27;s approach to the Space Shuttle and praised the engineering \(GMoromisato\); these are opinions and not independent evidence of long‑term operational success.

**「Impact」** Teams building collaboration and AI-notetaking features should treat defaults as a privacy risk: audit whether meetings are recorded or transcribed by default, add clear opt-in/opt-out controls, minimize remote retention and data fusion, and update privacy notices and compliance checks to reflect inferred-data uses. The announcement affects vendors, open‑source projects, and incident‑response teams that receive reports from Project Zero and must now anticipate faster public disclosure schedules.

**「Immediate consequence」** Vendors and OSS maintainers will have substantially less time to develop, test, and roll out coordinated fixes; security teams should treat Project Zero reports as high‑priority SLAs \(90, 60, then 30 days\) and prepare mitigations, backport plans, and communication in advance to avoid being caught by public disclosure.

### [Lovable apps exposed passport data and 200K+ private messages](https://www.crissyfield.de/research/lovable-en/) ⭐️ 8.0/10

A researcher’s blog post alleges that multiple apps built with Lovable exposed passport details and more than 200,000 private messages because row-level security \(RLS\) was insufficient or not enabled and platform defaults were permissive. The post blames weak RLS and permissive Supabase defaults used by those apps; Lovable’s Head of Security responded that Lovable scans apps for RLS issues, offers a SAST scan, and provides a one-click “Fix” plus an opt-in auto-fix and publish-blocking behavior when findings are flagged. The account is a researcher report that includes vendor statements and community commentary rather than independent third-party verification.

hackernews · armcat · Nov 14, 17:43 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50499017)

**「Row-level security and Supabase anon keys」** Row-level security \(RLS\) is a Postgres feature that enforces per-row access policies so client-side API keys cannot read or modify arbitrary table rows; hosted Postgres platforms \(for example, Supabase\) rely on RLS to prevent browsers from directly querying sensitive data. When RLS is disabled or defaults permit anonymous access via a public/&quot;anon&quot; key, browser clients can retrieve database rows without authentication — Crissy Field&\#x27;s scan of Lovable-built apps reported tens of thousands of projects exposing passport numbers, payment and other sensitive records including over 200,000 private messages, illustrating how permissive defaults plus missing RLS produce measurable mass exposure.

**「Impact」** Engineers who deploy apps with Lovable or Supabase-backed templates should immediately verify that Row-Level Security is enabled and that database policies follow a deny-by-default posture, audit any published apps for exposed personal data, and remediate misconfigurations; community reports also suggest checking whether historical projects retained permissive defaults even after vendor fixes. The incident highlights a concrete compatibility/action concern: fixes to platform defaults may not retroactively secure existing projects, so teams must explicitly review and update project-level settings rather than rely on a platform-wide remediation.

**「Community discussion」** The researcher \(author\) re-shared the post and offered to answer questions, while Lovable’s Head of Security described the company’s automated scanning and auto-fix features. Commenters debated culpability, with some \(e.g., SOLAR\_FIELDS\) pointing to Supabase’s permissive defaults and a reported October 30 fix that may not have changed existing projects, and others arguing the proliferation of easy-to-create/AI-generated apps increases the scale and exposure of such misconfigurations.

The update highlights metadata and signing/provenance indicators on a package&\#x27;s page, and has prompted discussion about adopting sigstore-style keyless signatures, required CI/OIDC support, and trade-offs around centralization and vendor lock‑in.

hackernews · rbanffy · Nov 14, 15:40 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50497892)

**「Why provenance and keyless signing matter」** The PyPI UI changes build on existing provenance and attestation features that record and display metadata about how a release was produced and which publisher or CI performed the upload, so the site can surface those signals directly on project pages \(the blog post announcing the redesign describes this as the work&\#x27;s goal\). A related technical approach is Sigstore-style “keyless” signing, where short‑lived certificates issued via OpenID Connect bind an identity to an artifact signature instead of relying on long‑lived private keys, letting systems prove who performed a build or release.

**「Practical consequence」** Developers and maintainers who want their releases to show favorable provenance indicators will likely need to publish provenance metadata or signatures and ensure their CI can perform keyless or OIDC‑based signing; CI/CD providers should expect demand for OIDC workflows to integrate with signing systems. Users should treat the signals as an added vetting aid rather than a guarantee: the visual indicators help surface provenance changes but do not by themselves detect every class of malicious package.

**「Community reaction」** Commenters split between enthusiasm for broader adoption of sigstore-style keyless signatures and concerns that relying on OIDC and hosted providers centralizes trust and risks lock‑in \(time4tea, samhclark\). Other remarks called for reusable/trusted publishing workflows to be supported by PyPI \(mhils\), showed concrete package examples of present/absent provenance displays \(djoldman\), and warned the approach mainly detects hijacked entries rather than inherently malicious projects \(cowlevel\).
